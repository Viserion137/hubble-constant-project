\begin{abstract}
This project estimates the Hubble constant $H_0$ using two independent datasets: Edwin Hubble's original 1929 sample of 24 extragalactic objects, and a modern sample of 39 galaxies with distances and redshifts drawn from the NASA/IPAC Extragalactic Database (NED) and, for the six galaxies with geometric maser distances, from the Megamaser Cosmology Project. Refitting Hubble's data with his own model, which included the motion of the Sun, reproduces his published value of 465 km/s/Mpc; the comparison with the modern sample shows how limitations in the extragalactic distance scale of the 1920s led to an overestimate of the expansion rate by a factor of about six. For the modern sample, all distances are first converted to comoving distance, since the maser distances are angular-diameter distances and the others luminosity distances: although no galaxy is beyond $z = 0.034$, this changes the result by about 2 km/s/Mpc, more than its statistical error. The reference fit gives $H_0 = 72.5 \pm 1.5$ km/s/Mpc, with jackknife and bootstrap cross-checks. A sensitivity analysis shows that the six maser galaxies reduce the uncertainty by a factor of 2.7 and lower the central value by an amount within the uncertainty of the maser-free fit. An inverse-variance weighted fit illustrates a common pitfall (weighting by distance precision alone reintroduces the very peculiar-motion bias the sample extension was designed to suppress) and is repeated with a peculiar-velocity scatter estimated from the data; an orthogonal distance regression additionally allows for measurement error on distance, and a Monte Carlo simulation shows that all three estimators are unbiased to within 0.3 km/s/Mpc. A paired bootstrap shows that the spread among the ten resulting estimators (69.6--74.9 km/s/Mpc) is comparable to their own sampling noise: no estimator differs from the reference fit by more than 1.6 times the noise of that difference. Finally, the weighted method is validated against the published Megamaser Cosmology Project result, which it reproduces to within 0.2$\sigma$.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
Hubble's Law is one of the foundational results of modern cosmology: it provided the first empirical evidence that the Universe is expanding, and remains central to how astronomers measure cosmic distances, ages, and the overall scale of the Universe. The value of the Hubble constant $H_0$ sets the present-day expansion rate of the Universe and is directly linked to its estimated age: a higher $H_0$ implies a younger Universe, and vice versa.
Hubble's Law states that the recession velocity $v$ of a galaxy is proportional to its distance $r$ from us:
\begin{equation}
v = H_0 \, r
\label{eq:hubble_law}
\end{equation}
where $H_0$ is the Hubble constant, typically expressed in km/s/Mpc.

It is worth being explicit about what Equation~\ref{eq:hubble_law} actually is: the leading-order term of the relation between distance and redshift $z$, valid for $z \ll 1$. Two details matter at the few-per-cent level reached in this project. First, ``distance'' can mean different things. A distance obtained by comparing the apparent and intrinsic brightness of a standard candle (Cepheids, the tip of the red giant branch, surface brightness fluctuations, supernovae) is a \emph{luminosity distance} $D_L$; a distance obtained by comparing the physical and angular size of an object (the maser disks of Section~\ref{sec:method2}) is an \emph{angular-diameter distance} $D_A$; the two differ by a factor $(1+z)^2$. Second, to the next order the relation gains a term involving the deceleration parameter $q_0$:
\begin{equation}
H_0 D_L \approx cz\left[1 + \tfrac{1}{2}(1-q_0)\,z\right], \qquad
H_0 D_A \approx cz\left[1 - \tfrac{1}{2}(3+q_0)\,z\right].
\label{eq:second_order}
\end{equation}
Every object in both samples has $v \ll c$ (at most $z \approx 0.034$, for the maser galaxy NGC 6264), but for that galaxy the correction to $D_A$ is still about 4\%, larger than the statistical error of the final result. For this reason the analysis of the modern sample converts every distance to comoving distance, for which Equation~\ref{eq:hubble_law} holds exactly (Section~\ref{sec:comoving}); no attempt is made to constrain $q_0$ itself from this data.

This project revisits the very first measurement of $H_0$, published by Edwin Hubble in 1929 \citep{hubble1929}, and compares it with a modern estimate obtained using the same statistical method but with contemporary distance measurements. The goal is not simply to reproduce a historical number, but to understand \emph{why} Hubble's original estimate differed so substantially from the value accepted today, and what this reveals about the evolution of observational astronomy over the past century. Hubble's own initial value, $H_0 \approx 500$ km/s/Mpc, was already revised upward within a few years, and in 1935 Arthur Eddington, combining relativity and quantum theory, confidently claimed to have found ``a solution which I regard as definitive,'' with $H_0 = 865$ km/s/Mpc \citep{tully2023} --- a reminder that overconfidence in a single method has a long history in this field, one this project tries to avoid by explicitly comparing two independent approaches, attaching uncertainties to every result, and being transparent about their respective limitations.
Despite nearly a hundred years of increasingly precise measurements, the exact value of $H_0$ remains a subject of active debate. Measurements based on the local Universe --- using distance-ladder methods such as Cepheids and Type Ia supernovae --- give $H_0 = 73.04 \pm 1.04$ km/s/Mpc \citep{riess2022}, while measurements inferred from the cosmic microwave background under the standard $\Lambda$CDM model give $H_0 = 67.4 \pm 0.5$ km/s/Mpc \citep{planck2020}. This discrepancy, known as the \emph{Hubble tension}, is quoted by the SH0ES team at about 5$\sigma$ significance --- meaning that the probability of it being merely a statistical fluctuation would be about 1 in 3.5 million. In 2026 a community-wide combination of many local distance indicators, the Local Distance Network, found $H_0 = 73.50 \pm 0.81$ km/s/Mpc, 7.1$\sigma$ from the early-Universe value obtained by combining Planck with ground-based CMB data under $\Lambda$CDM \citep{h0dn2026}. Whether the tension signals new physics beyond $\Lambda$CDM or unrecognized systematic errors in the local distance ladder is still debated. The most recent analysis of the Chicago-Carnegie Hubble Program (CCHP), which calibrates Type Ia supernovae with the Tip of the Red Giant Branch (TRGB) using both the Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescopes, finds an intermediate value, $H_0 = 70.39 \pm 1.22\text{ (stat)} \pm 1.33\text{ (sys)} \pm 0.70\,(\sigma_{\text{SN}})$ km/s/Mpc, which its authors describe as consistent with $\Lambda$CDM without the need for new physics \citep{freedman2025}, a conclusion that remains disputed. While a full treatment of this tension is beyond the scope of this project, it highlights that the precise determination of $H_0$ remains an open problem at the forefront of astrophysics --- one to which this project's own estimates, despite their modest scale, can be compared, provided their uncertainties are stated honestly.
\section{Part 1: Reproducing Hubble's Original Analysis (1929)}
\subsection{Data and Method}
I used the original dataset published by Hubble (1929), consisting of 24 extragalactic objects with measured distance $r$ (in megaparsecs) and radial velocity $v$ (in km/s). To estimate the Hubble constant, I first performed a linear fit of the form $v = H_0r$, using two approaches: an unconstrained linear regression (allowing a free intercept) and a regression forced through the origin, which is the physically motivated form of Hubble's Law, since a galaxy at zero distance should show zero recession velocity due to cosmic expansion. For every fit, I also report the standard error on $H_0$ from ordinary least squares and the coefficient of determination $R^2$, to quantify both the precision and the quality of the fit. Hubble's velocities, however, are heliocentric: they include the motion of the Sun itself, which Hubble solved for together with the expansion. Section~\ref{sec:hubble_model} therefore also reproduces his own model, for which the sky coordinates of the 24 objects were added (J2000, from the OpenNGC catalogue for the NGC objects, \citealt{openngc}, and from SIMBAD for the Magellanic Clouds, \citealt{simbad}).

\subsection{Hubble's own model: the motion of the Sun}
\label{sec:hubble_model}
The fits above differ from Hubble's in one respect. Hubble did not fit Equation~\ref{eq:hubble_law} alone: since the velocities of the nearest objects were of the same order as the motion of the Sun, he solved for the expansion term $K$ and for the three components $(X, Y, Z)$ of the solar motion together, through the equations of condition \citep{hubble1929}
\begin{equation}
v = K r + X\cos\alpha\cos\delta + Y\sin\alpha\cos\delta + Z\sin\delta,
\label{eq:hubble_solar}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ and $\delta$ are the right ascension and declination of each object. Solving Equation~\ref{eq:hubble_solar} by least squares on the same 24 objects gives the results in Table~\ref{tab:hubble_solar}.

The fit recovers Hubble's published expansion term, the direction and speed of the solar motion, and his statement that the ``residuals average 150 km./sec.'' The small difference in the quoted uncertainty of $K$ (55.7 against 50) was not investigated. Excluding the Local Group, the same model gives $K = 499 \pm 68$ km/s/Mpc. The difference between the 424 km/s/Mpc of the simple fit and Hubble's 465--500 is therefore entirely due to the solar motion, about 300 km/s, comparable to the velocities of the nearest objects themselves. Hubble also noted that the difference between his two solutions (465 for the 24 objects individually, $513 \pm 60$ for nine groups) was ``due largely to the four Virgo nebulae, which, being the most distant objects and all sharing the peculiar motion of the cluster, unduly influence the value of K'' \citep{hubble1929}: the same Virgo Cluster effect reappears in the modern nearby sample (Section~\ref{sec:discussion2}).

\subsection{Discussion}
Several of the nearest objects in the sample --- the Magellanic Clouds, NGC 6822, M33, M32, and M31 (Andromeda) --- belong to the Local Group, the small group of galaxies gravitationally bound to the Milky Way, where gravitational interactions between galaxies (peculiar motion) dominate over the recession caused by cosmic expansion. Some velocities in Table~\ref{tab:hubble1929_data} are negative: M31 ($-220$ km/s) and NGC 3031 (M81, $-30$ km/s). Much of this, however, is not the motion of the galaxies themselves but the reflex of the Sun's own orbit around the Milky Way, at about 250 km/s \citep{schonrich2010, mcmillan2011}. Along the line of sight to M31 this contributes $+192$ km/s, so that M31's modern heliocentric velocity of $-301$ km/s corresponds to a Galactocentric radial velocity of $-109$ km/s \citep{vandermarel2012}: M31 really is approaching the Milky Way, a genuine Local Group effect, but at about a third of its heliocentric speed. For NGC 3031, which belongs to the M81 group outside the Local Group, the reflex is $+115$ km/s, and Hubble's $-30$ km/s becomes a recession of about $+85$ km/s. This is precisely why Hubble's equations of condition included the solar motion.

Excluding the six Local Group members shifts the two simple fits in opposite directions: the free-intercept slope decreases (454 → 409 km/s/Mpc), while the origin-forced slope increases slightly (424 → 433 km/s/Mpc). Both shifts are comparable to, or smaller than, one standard error on the respective fit, so this asymmetry is only mildly suggestive rather than a strong effect; it is nonetheless consistent with the origin-forced fit being less sensitive to the distortion introduced by nearby peculiar motions, and is therefore treated here as the more reliable estimator in this context.
Notably, even after removing the Local Group, $H_0$ remains around 409--433 km/s/Mpc (499 with Hubble's model) --- still roughly six times higher than the modern value, and far outside the uncertainty of every fit. This indicates that neither peculiar motions nor the motion of the Sun can account for Hubble's overestimate. The dominant source of error was a systematic underestimate of the distances themselves, which the following decades corrected in several steps rather than all at once.

It helps first to recall how Hubble's distances were obtained \citep{hubble1929}. Only the first seven objects --- the six Local Group members and NGC 5457 --- had distances based on Cepheids and other recognizable stars. The next thirteen assumed that the brightest stars in every galaxy share a common upper limit of luminosity, $M_{\text{pg}} \approx -6.3$, calibrated on the nearby galaxies; the last four, members of the Virgo Cluster, were placed at a common distance of about 2 Mpc. Of the 18 objects that remain after excluding the Local Group, therefore, only NGC 5457 has a Cepheid-based distance.

The first correction came from Walter Baade's discovery of two populations of Cepheids with different luminosities \citep{baade1956}: the Cepheids in Hubble's calibrating galaxies are about 1.5 mag brighter, at a given period, than the calibration assumed, which increased extragalactic distances by about a factor of 2 \citep{tully2023} and would therefore roughly halve $H_0$. Because the brightest-star criterion was calibrated on the same nearby galaxies, this correction propagated to the whole sample --- but it accounts for only about a factor of 2 out of the factor of six. The second step came from \citet{humason1956}, who showed that many of the objects taken as the ``brightest stars'' in more distant galaxies were in fact HII regions, clouds of ionized gas around groups of hot stars, intrinsically much brighter than any single star; with this and the Cepheid correction they obtained $H_0 \approx 180$ km/s/Mpc. Two years later, \citet{sandage1958} revised the brightest-star calibration further and placed $H_0$ between about 50 and 100 km/s/Mpc, with a preferred value of 75 \citep{tully2023}. Hubble's factor of six is therefore the product of a chain of errors in the distance scale --- the Cepheid zero point first, then the misidentification of HII regions as stars and the recalibration of the brightest-star method --- and not of the Cepheid calibration alone. Unlike the statistical uncertainties quoted above, none of these errors can be corrected after the fact within this analysis, since they affect the distances themselves.
\subsection{Partial Conclusion}
Hubble's 1929 estimate of $H_0$ was overstated primarily because his distances were systematically too small, rather than because of the specific choice of galaxies in the sample or of the fitting method: with his own model, which includes the motion of the Sun, his published value is reproduced exactly. The underestimate came from a chain of errors in the distance scale of the time: the Cepheid zero point, corrected by Baade, accounts for about a factor of 2, and most of the remainder came from the later revision of the brightest-star distances, which were the majority of the sample, beginning with the recognition that many of Hubble's ``brightest stars'' were HII regions. This sets up a natural comparison with modern distance measurements in Part 2, using the NED catalogue \citep{ned}.
\section{Part 2: Modern Distance Estimates (NED)}
\subsection{Data and Method}
\label{sec:method2}
To compare Hubble's 1929 result with a modern estimate obtained using the same statistical approach, a sample of 39 galaxies outside the Local Group was selected, using distance and redshift data retrieved from the NASA/IPAC Extragalactic Database (NED). For each galaxy, the distance $r$ (in Mpc) was taken from the ``Distances'' entry, preferring Cepheid-based measurements where available. For galaxies without Cepheid data (mostly ellipticals), Surface Brightness Fluctuations (SBF) were used instead, and for the most distant part of the sample, geometric maser distances from the Megamaser Cosmology Project (MCP) \citep{pesce2020} were used, together with one Type Ia supernova (SNIa), one Type II supernova (SNII), and one Tip of the Red Giant Branch (TRGB) distance where no better method was available. These methods, together with their respective systematic uncertainties, are reviewed in detail in \citet{freedman2010}. Local Group members were deliberately excluded, applying the same methodological lesson drawn from Part 1. The sample was deliberately extended to include five MCP maser galaxies (UGC 3789, NGC 6264, NGC 5765b, NGC 6323, and CGCG 074-064), which reach distances of 51.5--132.1 Mpc and provide some of the most precise, geometric distance measurements available, independent of the traditional distance ladder. Together with NGC 4258, whose geometric maser distance anchors the distance ladder, they form the six maser-distance galaxies in the sample. NED lists several distance estimates for NGC 4258, including values of about 6.4 and 7.6 Mpc; the maser distance of 7.58 Mpc \citep{reid2019} was adopted, as the most precise available and the one on which modern Cepheid calibrations are anchored. The same criterion --- adopting the most recent and precise published determination --- was applied to the five MCP galaxies, whose distances are taken from the combined MCP analysis \citep{pesce2020}. That analysis re-modelled four of them (UGC 3789, NGC 6264, NGC 6323, and NGC 5765b) with revised position-error floors, superseding the earlier individual determinations (49.6, 144, 107, and about 126 Mpc respectively), and it also lists a geometric maser distance for CGCG 074-064 (87.6 Mpc), used here instead of the Fundamental Plane distance (74.4 Mpc) adopted in earlier versions of this project. For transparency, this update alone raised the origin-forced estimates of version 1.2.0 by 2.4 and 2.5 km/s/Mpc relative to version 1.1.0.

Five further distances were corrected in version 1.2.1 after checking them against the literature. NGC 4594 (the Sombrero galaxy) had been listed as a Cepheid distance of 6.22 Mpc, but no Cepheid distance exists for this galaxy: the value matches the lowest globular-cluster luminosity function estimate in the published range (6.22--21.7 Mpc; \citealt{mcquinn2016}), and it is replaced by the TRGB distance of $9.55 \pm 0.13\text{ (stat)} \pm 0.31\text{ (sys)}$ Mpc \citep{mcquinn2016}. For NGC 300, the adopted 1.49 Mpc was well below the Cepheid measurements, and it is replaced by the near-infrared Cepheid distance of 1.88 Mpc ($\mu_0 = 26.37$) of \citet{gieren2005}. For NGC 2403, NGC 3351, and NGC 3627, the adopted distances (2.88, 8.99, and 8.73 Mpc) were 0.08--0.18 mag shorter than the final HST Key Project Cepheid moduli, 27.48, 29.85, and 29.86 mag (without metallicity correction; \citealt{freedman2001}), and are replaced by the corresponding 3.13, 9.33, and 9.38 Mpc. Because these galaxies are all within 10 Mpc, where the fit gives them little weight, the five corrections changed the unweighted full-sample estimates by at most 0.22 km/s/Mpc; the remaining Cepheid distances were not re-sourced (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Table~\ref{tab:modern_data} reports the full sample used.

Two velocity frames were tested: the heliocentric radial velocity $v_{\text{helio}}$, and the velocity corrected for the Sun's motion relative to the cosmic microwave background, $v_{\text{CMB}}$, both taken from NED. The exception are the six maser galaxies, for which the systemic velocities measured from the maser disks themselves are used (Table~1 of \citealt{pesce2020}, CMB frame), the same values used in the validation of Section~\ref{sec:validation}; NED's velocities for these galaxies differ from them by up to 140 km/s (NGC 6323). Their heliocentric velocities are obtained by applying, for each galaxy, NED's own difference between the two frames. As in Part 1, two linear fits of the form $v = H_0 r$ were performed for each velocity frame: one with a free intercept, and one forced through the origin, again with standard errors and $R^2$ computed for each.

\subsection{From distances and redshifts to the Hubble law}
\label{sec:comoving}
Two kinds of distance enter the sample. Cepheid, TRGB, SBF and supernova distances come from distance moduli and are luminosity distances $D_L$. Maser distances come from the ratio between the physical and the angular size of the disk and are angular-diameter distances $D_A$; \citet{pesce2020} relate them to redshift accordingly (their Equation~1). In an expanding universe, Equation~\ref{eq:hubble_law} is exact for neither of them, but for the comoving distance $D_C$, provided the velocity is understood as
\begin{equation}
H_0 D_C = c\int_0^z \frac{dz'}{E(z')}, \qquad E(z) = \sqrt{\Omega_m (1+z)^3 + 1 - \Omega_m},
\label{eq:comoving}
\end{equation}
in a flat $\Lambda$CDM universe, with $z = v/c$ and $\Omega_m = 0.315$ \citep{planck2020}, as in \citet{pesce2020}. The distances convert as
\begin{equation}
D_C = \frac{D_L}{1+z} = D_A\,(1+z),
\label{eq:dc}
\end{equation}
and for $z \ll 1$ the right-hand side of Equation~\ref{eq:comoving} is $cz\left[1 - \tfrac{1}{2}(1+q_0)z\right]$, with $q_0 = \tfrac{3}{2}\Omega_m - 1 \approx -0.53$. The factor $(1+z)$ in Equation~\ref{eq:dc} depends on no cosmological model; only the much smaller term in $q_0$ does, and changing $\Omega_m$ from 0.25 to 0.35 moves the results below by at most 0.1 km/s/Mpc. All distances are converted with the CMB-frame redshift. Table~\ref{tab:comoving} lists the effect on the seven galaxies beyond 25 Mpc; for the other 32 it is at most 0.6\%.

\subsection{Weighted Fit}
\label{sec:weighted}
The fits above treat every galaxy as equally informative, regardless of how precisely its distance is known. As a first attempt at correcting this, each galaxy was assigned a representative fractional distance uncertainty based on its distance method, using the typical ranges summarized in \citet{freedman2010}: 7\% for Cepheids, 10\% for SBF, 8\% for SNIa, 15\% for SNII (optical), and 5\% for masers; the single TRGB distance is given 5\%, slightly more than its own quoted uncertainty of about 3.5\% \citep{mcquinn2016}. These are method-level, representative values, not individual NED-quoted errors for each object, and should be read as an order-of-magnitude correction rather than a precise error budget.

A first, naive attempt is instructive precisely because it fails: weighting each point by the distance-implied uncertainty on $v$ alone ($\sigma_v = H_0 \cdot f_{\text{method}} \cdot r$, propagated through an iteratively reweighted least-squares scheme) pulls the origin-forced fit to $H_0 = 80.6 \pm 4.4$ km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{helio}}$ and $94.8 \pm 7.8$ km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{CMB}}$ --- both well outside the accepted range, with a poor fit quality ($\chi^2/\text{dof} = 24$ and $55$ respectively). The reason is instructive: because $\sigma_v \propto r$ in this naive scheme, nearby galaxies are assigned artificially small uncertainties and therefore dominate the fit, even though it is precisely at small $r$ that peculiar motions (not distance-measurement error) are the dominant source of scatter. In effect, naive distance-only weighting reintroduces the same low-$r$, peculiar-velocity-dominated bias that the sample extension described in Section~\ref{sec:method2} was designed to suppress.

A more defensible weighting scheme therefore adds a distance-independent peculiar-velocity floor $\sigma_{\text{pec}}$ in quadrature, $\sigma_v = \sqrt{(H_0 \cdot f_{\text{method}} \cdot r)^2 + \sigma_{\text{pec}}^2}$, reflecting the fact that peculiar velocities of a few hundred km/s do not shrink simply because a galaxy's distance happens to be well measured. Rather than adopting a value for $\sigma_{\text{pec}}$, it is estimated from the data, as the value for which the origin-forced weighted fit has $\chi^2/\text{dof} = 1$: the scatter that the distance errors alone do not explain. This gives $\sigma_{\text{pec}} = 368$ km/s for $v_{\text{helio}}$ and $528$ km/s for $v_{\text{CMB}}$; a joint maximum-likelihood fit of $H_0$ and $\sigma_{\text{pec}}$ gives almost the same values (372 and 529 km/s, with $H_0 = 72.61$ and $74.65$ km/s/Mpc). Both are larger than the typical galaxy peculiar velocities of order 100--300 km/s relative to the Hubble flow. This is not surprising for this sample: several of its nearby galaxies lie in the region of the Virgo Cluster, whose members move at several hundred km/s relative to the cluster itself, and in the CMB frame the nearby galaxies also carry part of the motion of the Local Group (Section~\ref{sec:discussion2}). The choice of $\sigma_{\text{pec}}$ matters mainly in the CMB frame: a fixed floor of 150, 200 or 300 km/s gives 73.9, 73.3 and 72.8 km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{helio}}$, but 79.7, 77.8 and 76.0 km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{CMB}}$, with $\chi^2/\text{dof}$ between 1.4 and 8.8. The fixed floor of 200 km/s used up to version 1.2.1 gives $\chi^2/\text{dof} = 2.9$ and $5.7$: the fit itself indicates that such a floor is too small, and too small a floor gives the nearby galaxies, whose scatter it underestimates, too much weight, which pushes the CMB-frame estimate up. A single isotropic floor remains a simplification, since real peculiar motions are direction-dependent (e.g.\ infall toward the Virgo Cluster or the Great Attractor) and larger near clusters. The fitted value is set mostly by the nearby galaxies: the seven galaxies beyond 25 Mpc scatter around the reference fit by only about 300 km/s (root mean square), against about 500 km/s expected from the adopted error model.

As a complementary check, the unweighted fractional residuals, $(v - H_0 r)/(H_0 r)$, were also examined by method. Below, each group mean is quoted with its standard error ($s/\sqrt{n}$, where $s$ is the scatter of the group), and the scatter is given separately. The 20 Cepheid-based points have a positive mean residual of $+0.19 \pm 0.10$ (scatter 0.46), 1.9 standard errors from zero: they lie on average above the fit, as expected for nearby galaxies, whose velocities are strongly affected by local peculiar motions --- two of the three largest residuals ($+0.8$ to $+1.1$) belong to NGC 4535 and NGC 4536, in the region of the Virgo Cluster --- and possibly also because some of their distances are still somewhat short (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The 10 SBF points are consistent with zero ($-0.01 \pm 0.10$, scatter 0.31), and the 6 masers are both centered near zero and far tighter than either ($-0.04 \pm 0.03$, scatter 0.08). The single SNIa, SNII, and TRGB points cannot support a statistical statement on their own. This is consistent with, though not strong independent proof of, the geometric maser distances being the most internally precise method in the sample --- exactly why Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} below finds them so influential.

\subsubsection{A check on errors in both variables (ODR)}
Every fit so far, weighted or not, is a form of ordinary least squares: it assumes the distance $r$ is known exactly and puts all the measurement error on the velocity $v$. This is not quite right, since $r$ is, if anything, the less certain of the two quantities. Orthogonal Distance Regression (ODR) relaxes this assumption: instead of minimizing vertical distances to the fit line, it minimizes a weighted combination of the horizontal and vertical distances, using an error budget on \emph{both} axes. On the distance axis this is $\sigma_r = f_{\text{method}} \cdot r$; on the velocity axis it includes only the terms that genuinely belong to the velocity, namely the fitted peculiar-velocity floor and a representative measurement error of 5 km/s, added in quadrature. For a straight line through the origin, ODR is equivalent to minimizing
\begin{equation}
S(H_0) = \sum_i \frac{(v_i - H_0 r_i)^2}{\sigma_{v,i}^2 + H_0^2\,\sigma_{r,i}^2},
\end{equation}
so ODR itself propagates the distance error onto the velocity axis, and the effective variance of each point is the same combination used in the weighted fit. This form is used here; it gives the same results as the \texttt{scipy.odr} implementation of earlier versions, which SciPy has deprecated. Version 1.1.0 mistakenly also included the distance term $H_0 f_{\text{method}} r$ in $\sigma_v$, counting the distance error twice and biasing the ODR estimates upward; this was corrected in version 1.2.0.

The ODR gives $H_0 = 72.9 \pm 2.1$ km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{helio}}$ (compared to $72.7 \pm 2.1$ for the weighted fit) and $H_0 = 74.9 \pm 2.6$ km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{CMB}}$ (compared to $74.8 \pm 2.6$). The direction of the change is the expected one: ignoring measurement error on the independent variable causes ordinary least squares to systematically \emph{underestimate} the slope of a line, an effect statisticians call regression dilution or attenuation bias, so allowing for it pushes $H_0$ up. Here the effect is small (0.2 km/s/Mpc in both frames), well within one standard error.

\paragraph{Monte Carlo check of the estimators.} To test the estimators themselves, 1{,}000 samples were simulated with the same distances and distance methods as the real one, a true $H_0$ of 72 km/s/Mpc, Gaussian fractional distance errors $f_{\text{method}}$ and Gaussian peculiar velocities of 368 km/s. Ordinary least squares through the origin, the weighted fit and the ODR recover the input value with mean biases of $-0.29$, $-0.15$ and $+0.09$ km/s/Mpc respectively: negligible compared with their scatter, and in the order expected from attenuation bias. The scatter of the simulated estimates, 2.0--2.1 km/s/Mpc, is larger than the errors measured on the real data (1.4--1.5 km/s/Mpc for the reference fit, Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}), because a single floor of 368 km/s overstates the scatter of the distant galaxies, as noted above. The errors measured on the real data are therefore the more relevant ones, with the caveat that resampling a sample whose weight is concentrated in about six galaxies is itself approximate.

\subsubsection{Statistical error versus choice of method}
\label{sec:method_vs_noise}
The five estimators applied to each velocity frame (ordinary least squares with a free intercept or through the origin, the two weighted fits, and ODR) span $70.8$--$72.9$ km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{helio}}$ and $69.6$--$74.9$ km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{CMB}}$. \subsubsection{Validation against the published MCP result}
\label{sec:validation}
As an external check of the method itself, the weighted origin-forced fit of Section~\ref{sec:weighted} was applied to the six MCP galaxies using the distances, distance uncertainties (symmetrized) and CMB-frame velocities published in Table~1 of \citet{pesce2020}, converted to comoving quantities as in Section~\ref{sec:comoving}, with the same fixed 250 km/s peculiar-velocity uncertainty adopted there. It gives $H_0 = 73.1 \pm 3.0$ km/s/Mpc, compared with the published $73.9 \pm 3.0$ km/s/Mpc: a difference of 0.2$\sigma$. Without the conversion, the same fit gives $75.4 \pm 3.1$ km/s/Mpc, 0.4$\sigma$ above the published value. Versions up to 1.2.1 attributed that difference to the asymmetric distance likelihoods of the published analysis, but most of it came from treating the angular-diameter distances as if they were comoving distances, which \citet{pesce2020} account for in their Equation~1. The small remaining difference is expected from the symmetrized errors. Within its precision, the simple method used throughout this project reproduces a professional result.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion2}
The four unweighted modern estimates (70.0--74.1 km/s/Mpc) lie within the range spanned by current measurements, $H_0 \approx 67$--$74$ km/s/Mpc \citep{planck2020, riess2022, h0dn2026}, toward its upper end, in stark contrast with Hubble's 1929 results (409--454 km/s/Mpc, depending on fit type and sample). Since the fitting procedure applied here is identical to the one used in Part 1, this large improvement cannot be attributed to a change in statistical method: it must instead reflect the far greater accuracy of modern distance measurements compared to those available in 1929.
As in Part 1, the origin-forced and free-intercept fits differ by less than two standard errors in both velocity frames, the origin-forced fit lying slightly above; the high $R^2$ values (0.95--0.97) show that a single linear relation accounts for the great majority of the variance in the velocities.
The most notable result of this extension concerns the relationship between the two velocity frames. Repeating the same fits on the nearby galaxies alone ($r \le 25$ Mpc, $n=32$) shows that there, switching from heliocentric to CMB-frame velocities \emph{worsens} the estimate: the origin-forced fit moves from $75.4 \pm 5.2$ to $85.9 \pm 7.1$ km/s/Mpc, and the free-intercept fit from $62.0 \pm 12.6$ to $55.0 \pm 16.2$ km/s/Mpc. These nearby-only fits are also far less precise ($R^2 = 0.17$--$0.45$, against $0.95$--$0.97$ for the full sample), because at these distances peculiar velocities of a few hundred km/s are comparable to the expansion signal itself. Once the seven galaxies beyond 25 Mpc are added (the five MCP masers, at 51.5--132.1 Mpc, together with NGC 7619 and NGC 2986), this discrepancy nearly disappears: the heliocentric and CMB-frame fits now agree to within about 1.5 km/s/Mpc (71.3 vs.\ 70.0 for the free intercept; 72.5 vs.\ 74.1 forced through the origin). A likely explanation is that the CMB frame is the wrong reference for nearby galaxies. The correction to the CMB frame removes the Sun's motion with respect to the CMB, most of which is the motion of the Local Group as a whole; the nearby galaxies largely share that motion, being carried by the same large-scale flows, in addition to the more local pull of the Virgo Cluster, so for them the correction adds a velocity they do not have relative to us. In the true Hubble flow these motions become comparatively negligible, and the two frames agree. Testing this directly would require velocities in the Local Group frame or corrected with a model of the local flows (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Hubble himself had already noted that the Virgo galaxies, sharing the cluster's motion, unduly influenced his value of $K$ (Section~\ref{sec:hubble_model}).

\paragraph{How much do the six maser galaxies matter?}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
Because the argument above rests on extending the sample to large distances, and because the five MCP galaxies (at 51.5--132.1 Mpc) are the points that reach farthest into the Hubble flow, it is worth asking directly how much the maser-distance galaxies, specifically, are driving the result. Repeating the origin-forced fit with all six of them removed (NGC 4258 included; $n=33$) gives $H_0 = 74.6 \pm 4.1$ km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{helio}}$ and $H_0 = 80.0 \pm 5.8$ km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{CMB}}$. Removing them therefore raises the central value by 2.1 and 5.9 km/s/Mpc respectively, 0.5 and 1.0 times the standard error of the maser-free fit, and increases the uncertainty by a factor of 2.7. The masers set the precision of the result, by providing a long, precise lever arm; they also pull its central value down, but by no more than about one standard error of the fit without them. Earlier versions of this project (up to 1.1.0) found that removing the masers raised $H_0$ by about 10 km/s/Mpc; that shift came largely from CGCG 074-064, which with its Fundamental Plane distance of 74.4 Mpc implied $v/r \approx 93$ km/s/Mpc, whereas it is now counted, with its geometric distance, among the maser galaxies. A complementary leave-one-out (jackknife) test, in which each galaxy is removed one at a time, shows that no single galaxy matters much: removing any one of them shifts the origin-forced $H_0$ by at most 0.8 km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{helio}}$ and 1.2 km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{CMB}}$ (NGC 6323 in both cases). The jackknife standard error (1.3 km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{helio}}$, 1.7 for $v_{\text{CMB}}$) and the standard deviation of a bootstrap resampling of the full sample (20{,}000 resamples; 1.4 and 2.0 km/s/Mpc) agree reasonably with the analytic standard errors in Table~\ref{tab:modern_results} (1.5 and 2.1 km/s/Mpc), and the bootstrap 68\% confidence intervals are $[71.3, 73.7]$ km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{helio}}$ and $[72.6, 75.9]$ km/s/Mpc for $v_{\text{CMB}}$. Taken together, these checks indicate that the result is a property of the extended sample as a whole --- specifically, of reaching into the true Hubble flow --- rather than an artifact of one favorably-placed data point.

As a further check, the case of M31 (Andromeda) was examined with its modern velocity. Its heliocentric velocity, $-301$ km/s, is still negative, as in Hubble's data, but as discussed in Part 1 about two thirds of it is the reflex of the Sun's orbit in the Milky Way: in the Galactocentric frame M31 approaches at $-109$ km/s \citep{vandermarel2012}. The approach itself is a genuine dynamical effect of the Local Group, where the mutual gravity of the Milky Way and M31 dominates over cosmic expansion --- the same kind of effect, on a larger scale, as the peculiar velocities that distort the nearby subsample above.

Finally, Figure~\ref{fig:h0_comparison} compares the origin-forced estimates with the literature. Using statistical errors only (the bootstrap standard deviation of each estimator combined with the quoted literature error), the reference estimate, $72.5 \pm 1.5$ km/s/Mpc, lies 0.3$\sigma$ from the SH0ES value \citep{riess2022}, 0.6$\sigma$ from the Local Distance Network \citep{h0dn2026}, 0.9$\sigma$ from the CCHP TRGB value \citep{freedman2025}, and 3.5$\sigma$ above the Planck value \citep{planck2020}. Across all ten estimators of Table~\ref{tab:method_vs_noise}, the distance from SH0ES ranges from $-1.2\sigma$ to $+0.7\sigma$ and from the Local Distance Network from $-1.4\sigma$ to $+0.6\sigma$, while the distance from Planck ranges from 0.9$\sigma$ to 3.5$\sigma$; the four free-intercept variants lie between 0.9$\sigma$ and 2.1$\sigma$ from Planck. This places the project's result on the local side of the tension, consistent with all three local measurements but unable to distinguish between them: the CCHP and SH0ES values differ by 2.7 km/s/Mpc, more than the statistical error of this estimate. Nor should the result be read as independent support for the local side. The measurement is built entirely on local distance indicators. Its most important ingredient, the maser distances, are the same ones from which \citet{pesce2020} derive their own local value of $73.9 \pm 3.0$ km/s/Mpc; NGC 4258 is the geometric anchor of the SH0ES Cepheid calibration; and choices about the data have moved the result by amounts comparable to its statistical error: by $+2.5$ km/s/Mpc when the updated maser distances were adopted (version 1.2.0), and by $-2.1$ km/s/Mpc when the distances were converted to comoving distance (this version). The comparison therefore reproduces, on a small scale, the same discrepancy between local and early-Universe determinations that defines the tension, rather than shedding light on its cause. It is also a useful reminder that ``landing near a published value'' and ``being a precise, well-controlled measurement'' are not the same claim, and this project is more confidently the former than the latter.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several limitations of this analysis should be acknowledged. First, the galaxy sample was not selected through a systematic or blind procedure, but by prioritizing objects with well-documented, high-quality distance measurements on NED; this could introduce a mild selection bias favoring galaxies that are easier to measure precisely. Second, the weighted fit in Section~\ref{sec:weighted} uses representative, method-level fractional errors rather than the individual quoted uncertainty of each galaxy (for the maser galaxies in particular, the single 5\% value is a simplification: the individual uncertainties published by \citet{pesce2020} and \citet{reid2019} range from about 1.5\% for NGC 4258 to about 26\% for NGC 6323), and its peculiar-velocity floor, although now estimated from the data, is a single isotropic number for all galaxies: it is set mostly by the nearby galaxies and overstates the scatter of the distant ones. Third, only two velocity frames (heliocentric and CMB) were tested; a more complete treatment would use velocities corrected with a model of the local flows (Virgo Cluster infall, the Great Attractor, and others), rather than relying on sample extension and a scatter floor to suppress their effect. For the maser galaxies alone, \citet{pesce2020} find that different treatments of peculiar velocities move $H_0$ between 71.8 and 76.9 km/s/Mpc, so this is a real source of systematic uncertainty. Fourth, as the sensitivity analysis in Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} makes explicit, the precision of the result depends on a small number of long-baseline galaxies (above all the five MCP masers: the effective number of galaxies in the fit is about six), and its central value depends on choices about their data: adopting the distances of \citet{pesce2020} moved the estimates by about $+2.5$ km/s/Mpc, and treating them correctly as angular-diameter distances by $-2.1$ km/s/Mpc. This is not a flaw in the method, but it does mean the modern estimate here is less independent of any single distance-measurement technique than a large professional sample would be. Fifth, the Cepheid distances, taken from NED, do not come from a single homogeneous calibration: the four that were checked against the literature in version 1.2.1 had to be replaced (Section~\ref{sec:method2}), and the other 16 have not been re-sourced from a single reference such as the final HST Key Project analysis \citep{freedman2001}. Because these galaxies all lie within about 21 Mpc, where the fit gives them little weight, this mainly affects the nearby-subsample fits and the Cepheid residuals rather than the full-sample estimate. Sixth, the conversion to comoving distance assumes a flat $\Lambda$CDM cosmology; this matters little, since the model-independent factor $(1+z)$ dominates and changing $\Omega_m$ from 0.25 to 0.35 moves the results by at most 0.1 km/s/Mpc. Finally, the sample size ($n=39$), while sufficient to obtain a stable estimate of $H_0$, remains small compared to the hundreds or thousands of objects used in professional determinations of the Hubble constant, so the results presented here should be understood as a stable proof-of-concept reproduction of a well-established method rather than a new independent constraint on $H_0$.
\section{Conclusion}
Reproducing Hubble's original 1929 analysis --- including, as he did, the motion of the Sun --- recovers his published value of $K = 465$ km/s/Mpc, an estimate of $H_0$ roughly six times higher than the modern accepted value, primarily because the distances of the time were systematically too small: the result of a chain of errors, from the Cepheid zero point (about a factor of 2) to the misidentification of HII regions as the brightest stars in more distant galaxies (compounded, to a lesser extent, by the inclusion of nearby galaxies whose peculiar motion is not representative of cosmic expansion). Applying the same statistical method to an extended modern sample of 39 galaxies, spanning distances from about 1.9 Mpc to 132 Mpc and using a combination of Cepheid, SBF, supernova, TRGB and geometric maser distances, yields unweighted $H_0$ estimates of $70.0$--$74.1$ km/s/Mpc, with statistical errors of 1.5--2.5 km/s/Mpc. The reference fit (heliocentric velocities, through the origin) gives $72.5 \pm 1.5$ km/s/Mpc: on the local side of the Hubble tension and consistent with all current local measurements, as expected for a measurement built entirely on local distance indicators. Reaching this required a correction that is easy to overlook: although every galaxy has $z < 0.035$, the maser distances are angular-diameter distances, and fitting them directly against redshift would have raised the result by about 2 km/s/Mpc, more than its statistical error. Jackknife and bootstrap resampling support the analytic uncertainties, and show that the six maser-distance galaxies set the precision of the result, by providing a long, geometric lever arm into the Hubble flow, while moving its central value by about one standard error of the fit without them, or less. Inverse-variance weighting, with a peculiar-velocity floor estimated from the data, and orthogonal distance regression move the estimate within $69.6$--$74.9$ km/s/Mpc depending on velocity frame and estimator; a Monte Carlo simulation shows that these estimators are unbiased, and a paired bootstrap shows that their differences are comparable to their own sampling noise (at most 1.6 times the noise of each difference), so with 39 galaxies the choice of method cannot be separated from statistical noise. The weighted method itself reproduces the published MCP value to within 0.2$\sigma$. If anything, the most important lesson of the project is that a defensible error budget, not just a best-fit number, is what turns a curve fit into a measurement --- and that this includes errors in the analysis itself: successive versions corrected a double counting of the distance uncertainty, five individual distances that were mislabelled or outdated, a fixed peculiar-velocity floor that the data did not support, and the treatment of angular-diameter distances, the last of which alone moved the result by about 2 km/s/Mpc. Taken together, the comparison illustrates that the key advance separating Hubble's original measurement from the modern value of $H_0$ was not a change in statistical methodology, but nearly a century of improvement in the precision of the extragalactic distance scale --- together with the importance of sampling galaxies far enough into the Hubble flow that local peculiar motions no longer bias the result, and of being honest about how much any single analysis choice can still move the answer.
